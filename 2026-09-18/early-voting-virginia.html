Virginia · elections · 18 September 2026

Early in-person voting for the 3 November 2026 midterms begins in Virginia.

ObjectiveNews. Target T 100 · N 0 · C 100.

What is agreed across desks

The Virginia Department of Elections announced that early in-person voting for the 3 November 2026 general election began on Friday, 18 September 2026. In localities whose general registrar offices would be closed on Saturday the 19th, the first day of the period was advanced to Friday so that voters would not lose a day of access. The official 45-day early-voting window continues through Saturday, 31 October. Registered voters may cast ballots at designated early-voting locations in their locality upon presentation of an acceptable form of identification listed in the Code of Virginia.

Contests on the ballot include a United States Senate seat, all eleven Virginia seats in the U.S. House of Representatives, three proposed amendments to the state constitution, and various local races and referendums that depend on the locality. New for 2026 is a statutory requirement that early voting be offered on the second and third Sundays before Election Day in addition to the existing Saturday availability. This change was enacted in prior legislative sessions and is now operational for the first time in a midterm cycle.

State election officials and multiple local registrars confirmed that sites opened on schedule. No widespread system outages or equipment failures were reported in the first hours of voting. The Department of Elections maintains a public list of early-voting locations and accepted identification documents; that list is the primary administrative source for the fact of the opening.

What is only a quote or interpretive framing

Some secondary coverage links the early-vote opening to ongoing federal litigation over mail-ballot and absentee rules. Those court cases address executive and legislative measures that would have altered the handling of mail ballots or citizenship verification for certain categories of voters. The Supreme Court and lower federal courts have issued rulings that blocked or delayed certain of those measures. Those rulings remain separate from the administrative fact that in-person early voting has opened under existing Virginia statute. Framing the early-vote start as a direct consequence of any single federal order goes beyond the text of the state announcement.

Partisan statements that treat the first day of early voting as an early indicator of turnout or as evidence of “voter enthusiasm” are interpretive. Turnout percentages become measurable only after the close of the early period and the Election Day canvass. Early numbers released by individual localities on the first day are partial and not yet comparable to prior cycles on a statewide basis.

Mechanism and history a reader needs

Virginia expanded no-excuse early voting in successive election cycles after 2020. The 45-day window is fixed by state law. Local general registrars administer the physical locations; the State Board of Elections and the Department of Elections publish guidance, site lists, and accepted forms of identification. Acceptable ID includes a Virginia driver’s license, passport, student ID from a Virginia institution, and several other documents listed in statute. Voters without photo ID may still vote after signing an affirmation of identity under procedures that have been in place for multiple cycles.

Curbside voting is available for voters with disabilities and for those age 65 or older who request it. Voters may also request a ballot by mail under separate absentee procedures; those procedures are the subject of the federal litigation mentioned above and are not identical to in-person early voting. Same-day registration is not available in Virginia; a voter must be registered in the locality where the ballot is cast by the statutory deadline.

Historically, midterm elections produce lower overall turnout than presidential years. Early voting as a share of total ballots has risen in Virginia and nationally since the expansion of no-excuse options. Whether the 2026 midterms follow that trend will be known only after the final canvass. The addition of two mandatory Sunday voting days is a structural change whose effect on participation can be measured by comparing Sunday volumes in 2026 with Saturday volumes in prior midterms.

The three constitutional amendments on the ballot address specific questions that the General Assembly placed before the voters. Their text and the official explanations are published by the Department of Elections. Passage requires a majority of votes cast on each amendment. Local races and referendums vary by county and city and are governed by local charters and state election law.

What the story is not

The opening of early voting is not a prediction of final turnout, partisan outcome, or the eventual resolution of mail-ballot litigation. It does not change voter-registration deadlines, the requirement that a voter be registered in the locality of the ballot, or the list of accepted identification. It is an administrative milestone reported by the state agency charged with conducting elections. Claims that the first day of early voting already demonstrates either widespread fraud or widespread suppression lack supporting data at this stage of the process.

Coverage that treats the early-vote start primarily as a political scoreboard for the midterm campaign misses the operational fact: sites opened under existing law, identification rules remain those published by the Department of Elections, and the 45-day period runs to 31 October regardless of the tone of campaign statements.
