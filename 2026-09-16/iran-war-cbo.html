Washington · defense spending · 15 September 2026

The Congressional Budget Office estimated Iran conflict costs at $38 billion through 1 August.

ObjectiveNews. Target T 100 · N 0 · C 100.

What is agreed across desks

On 15 September 2026 the Congressional Budget Office released an analysis stating that Department of Defense costs associated with combat operations against Iran totaled approximately $38 billion through 1 August 2026. The office projected that ongoing monthly costs could range from $2 billion to $3 billion or more depending on the intensity of operations. The largest single category was the cost of replacing expended munitions, estimated at $21.7 billion, including substantial outlays for missile-defense interceptors and land-attack cruise missiles.

The CBO noted that the Department of Defense did not respond to its requests for information, so the estimate rests on public databases and published reports. The figure is close to the $37.5 billion figure that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had provided in Senate testimony in July. The analysis excludes costs of repairing damaged bases, long-term personnel medical care, and certain future maintenance of equipment.

What is only a quote

Political characterizations of the report as proof that the war is unaffordable, or conversely that it is a necessary investment, are statements of viewpoint. The CBO document itself is a cost accounting, not a policy recommendation.

Mechanism and context

The CBO is a nonpartisan congressional agency charged with providing budget and economic information. Its cost estimates for military operations typically include the incremental expenses of munitions, fuel, flying hours, and equipment replacement beyond peacetime baselines. Munitions drawdown is particularly significant because production rates for advanced interceptors are limited; the CBO observed that replenishing inventories could take at least five years. Energy-price effects from restricted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz are treated separately as macroeconomic impacts that could raise measured inflation.

Earlier Inspector General reporting had placed costs through late June at roughly $33 billion, consistent with the trajectory that reaches $38 billion by early August. The White House had earlier requested a larger supplemental appropriation that included funding related to the conflict; Congress had not enacted that request by the date of the CBO release.

What this story is not

It is not an independent audit of every dollar spent. It is not a complete accounting of human or strategic costs. It is not a forecast of how long the conflict will last or of the ultimate total. Readers should treat the monthly run-rate as conditional on intensity remaining within the observed range.
