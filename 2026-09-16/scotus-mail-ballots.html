Washington · elections · 14–15 September 2026

The Supreme Court left in place a lower-court injunction blocking portions of a USPS mail-ballot rule before the midterms.

ObjectiveNews. Target T 100 · N 0 · C 100.

What is agreed across desks

On 14 September 2026 the Supreme Court issued an unsigned order denying the Trump administration’s emergency application for a stay of a preliminary injunction issued by a federal district judge in Massachusetts. The injunction prevents the United States Postal Service from implementing certain provisions of a new rule governing the delivery of mail-in ballots in time for the November 2026 midterm elections. Justice Brett Kavanaugh filed a concurrence stating that, even if the rule might ultimately be within the Postal Service’s authority, state and local election officials lacked sufficient time to implement it before the elections. Justices Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas dissented.

As a practical result, existing state procedures for mail ballots remain in force for the 2026 cycle. The order does not resolve the underlying legality of the rule on the merits; it addresses only the emergency stay request under the Court’s equitable standards for interim relief.

What is only a quote

Characterizations of the ruling as a definitive “victory” or “defeat” for any political program are interpretive. The Court’s order itself is limited to the stay application and the timing concerns highlighted by Justice Kavanaugh.

Mechanism and context

The administration had directed the Postal Service, pursuant to an executive order, to adopt requirements intended to limit ballot delivery to verified lists of eligible voters. A district judge found that the rule raised substantial legal questions under federal election law and the Constitution’s allocation of election administration primarily to the states, and that implementing it close to an election risked disenfranchisement. The Supreme Court’s emergency docket applies a multi-factor test that includes likelihood of success on the merits and the balance of equities. The majority concluded the government was unlikely to succeed and that the equities did not favor a stay.

Mail voting has been used by a substantial fraction of the electorate in recent cycles. Changes to procedural rules near an election are frequently scrutinized by courts for the risk of disruption. The concurrence leaves open the possibility that a similar rule could be examined on a different timeline in a later election cycle.

What this story is not

It is not a final judgment on the constitutionality or statutory authority of the Postal Service rule. It is not a finding that mail ballots are or are not secure. It is not a prediction of the outcome of the midterm elections or of any particular state’s vote-counting procedures.
