New York · courts · 12 October 2026

A federal judge ordered the Trump administration to halt remaining taxpayer-funded television ads promoting the president.

ObjectiveNews. Target T 100 · N 0 · C 100.

What is agreed across desks

On Sunday evening, 11 October 2026, U.S. District Judge Jennifer L. Rochon of the Southern District of New York issued a temporary restraining order directing the Trump administration to cancel any remaining airings of television advertisements paid for with taxpayer funds that promote President Donald Trump. The order applies for two weeks and specifically covers placements scheduled for that evening. Reuters, AP, Bloomberg, The Hill, and NBC News report the same core facts: the ads had cost approximately $19–20 million drawn from Department of Homeland Security funds, the administration had indicated the campaign was ending that night, and the judge found the dispute was not moot.

The lawsuit was brought by Common Cause, a government watchdog, along with unions and others. Plaintiffs argued the ads constituted illegal use of appropriated funds for political propaganda rather than public service announcements, particularly in the weeks before the 3 November midterm elections. The Justice Department filed that the campaign was already concluding, but Judge Rochon, a Biden appointee, ruled that plaintiffs were likely to succeed on the merits, would suffer irreparable harm, and that the public interest favored the injunction. She ordered that defendants shall not enter into, renew, or expand any agreement authorizing further development, creation, dissemination, placement, or airing of the challenged advertisements during the two-week period.

White House statements and earlier filings indicated that going forward the ads would be funded by the president’s political action committee rather than taxpayers, and that no reimbursement of prior spending was planned. The ads ended with a disclaimer that they were paid for by the U.S. government. Multiple outlets note bipartisan criticism of the original funding approach, including from at least one Senate Republican.

What is only a quote

Common Cause President Virginia Kase Solomón said the group was pleased the court acted to prevent more taxpayer dollars from being used for “blatantly unlawful political propaganda” and would continue until the administration is held fully accountable. That is a plaintiff’s characterization. It is not a finding by the court that the ads were unlawful in final judgment; it is a temporary order based on likelihood of success.

President Trump described the ads as a “positive promotion for our great U.S.A.” That is his framing of the content. It does not establish that the expenditure complied with federal restrictions on publicity or propaganda.

The Justice Department’s Saturday filing that the offending conduct would end the next day is a representation to the court. The judge determined it did not make the request for a temporary order unnecessary.

Mechanism a reader needs

Federal law restricts the use of congressionally appropriated funds for “publicity or propaganda.” Agencies may communicate about their programs, but using public money to promote a sitting president’s personal or partisan message, especially near an election, has long drawn scrutiny. The Department of Homeland Security drew the funds from a package related to immigration enforcement. Legal experts cited in coverage suggested possible violations of the publicity-or-propaganda restriction and other statutes governing appropriated funds.

A temporary restraining order is an emergency, short-term measure. It preserves the status quo while the court considers a longer preliminary injunction. It does not decide the ultimate legality of the ads or require refunds of money already spent. The two-week duration gives the administration time to respond and the plaintiffs time to develop the case. If the government wishes to resume taxpayer-funded versions, it must return to court.

The timing sits in the final weeks before the midterm elections. Courts have historically treated last-minute political communications funded by the government as particularly sensitive because they can affect voter information and the appearance of official endorsement. The administration’s decision to shift future funding to a PAC removes the immediate appropriations issue for new ads, but does not erase the prior expenditure or the court’s temporary bar on any remaining taxpayer placements.

Multiple similar lawsuits exist, including one by the Democratic National Committee in another district. Rochon’s order is the first reported judicial block. Parallel cases may produce additional rulings or consolidations. The precise statutory theory—whether the ads fall outside authorized public information, violate the Anti-Deficiency Act, or cross other lines—will be litigated on fuller briefing.

Readers should distinguish between the content of the ads and the funding source. Political parties and candidates routinely run promotional spots. The contested element is the use of DHS-appropriated dollars and the government disclaimer. The court has not ordered the president’s PAC to stop; it has ordered the government to stop using public funds for the challenged spots.

What this story is not

It is not a final determination that the ads were illegal. It is not a criminal investigation or an order that anyone be prosecuted. It is not a finding about the accuracy of the messages in the ads. It is not a ban on all government communications or on privately funded political advertising.
