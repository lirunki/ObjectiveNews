What AI executives actually proposed this weekend—and what they did not

A reconstruction of public statements, not a verdict on catastrophe scenarios.

On Saturday 12 September, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published an essay of about 3,800 words. The documented core is not “stop building AI.” It is: capability is compounding faster than evaluation; models are beginning to help build the next models; labs should slow the rate of capability jumps so evaluation can catch up.

Three steps, as reported by the New York Times, BBC and The Verge: (1) independent “embedded evaluators” inside labs, with publication of unflattering findings—Anthropic said it would start; (2) common safety standards and a capability speed limit among firms in democratic states; (3) attempt analogous limits that also bind China, treated as least likely near-term.

Sam Altman posted that he agreed a slowdown was needed. Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis voiced agreement with the direction. No lab announced a numerical freeze on training FLOPs. In a Fortune interview, Altman said OpenAI will not list in 2026: “Right now would be an ill-advised moment to go public.” Earlier 2026 reporting also described a $1 trillion valuation target and a rocky SpaceX debut as reasons a listing might slip. Both can be true; only the safety line was the one Altman chose this weekend.

Speaker Johnson said AI leaders should brief Congress and resisted a moratorium. President Trump called catastrophe forecasts “things that won’t happen” and said “whoever wins AI, wins.” Those are risk appetites, not technical papers.

Separate: (1) Did they say the quoted words? Yes. (2) Are models about to become uncontrollably self-improving? Contested; this weekend produced advocacy, not a new demonstration. (3) Does delaying an IPO equal a safety pause? No.
