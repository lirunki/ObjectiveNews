United States · courts · 19 October 2026

The Supreme Court declined to hear appeals seeking to revive 2024 plea agreements for Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and two co-defendants.

ObjectiveNews. Target T 100 · N 0 · C 100.

What is agreed across desks

On Monday, 19 October, the Supreme Court of the United States declined to hear appeals by Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, Mustafa al-Hawsawi, and Walid bin Attash. The three men, held at Guantánamo Bay and charged before a military commission in connection with the 11 September 2001 attacks, had sought to reinstate plea agreements reached in 2024. Under those agreements they would have pleaded guilty and received life sentences, taking the death penalty off the table. Then-Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin revoked the agreements two days after they were accepted by the convening authority. Military courts initially held that Austin lacked authority to withdraw the deals because performance had begun. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, in a 2-1 decision in July 2025, reversed and held that Austin “indisputably had legal authority to withdraw from the agreements.” The Supreme Court denied the petitions for certiorari without a written opinion. Justice Brett Kavanaugh did not participate in the consideration of the Mohammed and al-Hawsawi petition.

Reuters, CBS News, Bloomberg, CNN, and The Hill reported the denial and the underlying procedural history in matching terms. The practical effect is that the military commission case returns to the pretrial track with the death penalty remaining available. Prosecutors have previously decided not to seek reinstatement of certain confessions obtained after CIA custody. A trial date in 2028 has been referenced in related coverage.

What is only a quote

Statements by defense counsel or victims’ groups that the denial “clears the way” for trial or that it “denies closure” are advocacy. The Court’s order itself is the denial of review; it does not decide the merits of Austin’s authority beyond leaving the D.C. Circuit ruling in place. Claims that the defendants’ confessions were coerced are the subject of separate military-judge rulings and are not resolved by this certiorari denial. Assertions about the political context of Austin’s revocation in 2024 are historical framing, not part of the Supreme Court’s unsigned order.

Mechanism a reader needs

Military commissions at Guantánamo operate under the Military Commissions Act. Plea agreements require acceptance by the convening authority. The Secretary of Defense, as the superior authority, asserted the power to withdraw an agreement before substantial performance. The D.C. Circuit’s majority treated that power as clear; the dissent disagreed on timing and authority. The Supreme Court’s refusal to grant certiorari is not a holding on the merits. It simply means fewer than four justices voted to hear the case. Denials of certiorari have no precedential effect, but they leave the lower-court judgment intact.

The 9/11 case has been delayed for more than two decades by disputes over evidence obtained in CIA black sites, by successive administrations’ approaches to the commissions, and by the practical difficulty of trying capital cases with classified material. A plea that removes the death penalty can accelerate resolution and produce a factual record through the defendants’ statements. Withdrawal of that option returns the case to the slower path of pretrial litigation and, eventually, trial. Whether the 2028 target date holds depends on remaining evidentiary motions, including those concerning the admissibility of statements made at Guantánamo after transfer from CIA custody.

Victims’ families are divided: some preferred the certainty of a life sentence and a detailed account; others insisted on the possibility of a death sentence after trial. Neither preference is a legal argument before the Supreme Court. The legal question presented was whether the D.C. Circuit correctly interpreted the Secretary’s authority and whether that court had jurisdiction to review the military-commission rulings.

What this story is not

It is not a determination of guilt. It is not a ruling that the plea agreements were invalid on their face. It is not a statement about the conditions of confinement or the propriety of military commissions. The photograph above is a type exterior of the Supreme Court Building. It is not an image of Guantánamo or of the defendants.
