United States · aviation · 16 November 2026

FAA says recovery from the 13 November ground stop is advancing; Duffy continues to seek large modernization funding.

ObjectiveNews. Target T 100 · N 0 · C 100.

What is agreed across desks

On 13 November 2026 at approximately 3:30 p.m. Eastern Time the Federal Aviation Administration experienced a technical problem that led it to issue a nationwide ground stop. The FAA’s own questions-and-answers page states that the stop was issued out of an abundance of caution, that safety remained the highest priority, and that the system was stabilized so that air traffic could resume by 8:15 p.m. the same day. Approximately 3,500 aircraft that were already airborne landed safely. Operators canceled roughly 12,462 flights that day, including about 9,082 by major airlines.

Officials have described the proximate cause as a flood of data into the FAA’s legacy telecommunications network. A contractor, L3Harris, was performing routine work converting copper lines to fiber at a remote site. One or more routers began generating roughly 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds. The overload produced instability in radar and communications feeds at multiple Air Route Traffic Control Centers. The FAA has said there is no evidence of a cyberattack. Similar functions on other routers were later disabled.

By the weekend of 15–16 November, FlightAware and airline reports showed cancellations falling from several thousand on Saturday and Sunday into the low hundreds on Monday morning. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has said schedules should return to normal by the start of the week. He has also continued to argue that the underlying infrastructure is decades old and has asked Congress for emergency funding on the order of $50 billion to modernize it.

What is only a quote

Duffy’s statements that the problem is “old equipment” and that previous administrations failed to modernize the system are political characterizations. They are not engineering findings. The precise software or hardware defect inside the router, any contractor procedural error, and the long-term reliability of the remaining copper segments have not yet been published in a completed investigation report. Claims by any politician that the outage proves a particular budget philosophy are opinions, not the FAA timeline.

Mechanism a reader needs

The National Airspace System moves several tens of thousands of flights a day. Controllers rely on radar tracks, flight-plan data, and voice or data-link communications that travel over a mix of modern fiber and older copper circuits managed under the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure program. When a device on that network begins to retransmit connection requests at high volume, the routers that forward traffic can become saturated. Controllers then see intermittent radar or radio, which is unacceptable for separation. The ground stop is the procedural way to stop new departures while airborne aircraft continue to their destinations under existing clearances or contingency procedures.

Recovery is slower than the stop itself. Aircraft and crews end up out of position. Airlines must rebuild rotations, rebook passengers, and reposition equipment. That is why thousands of cancellations continued for two more days even after the network was stable. The numbers reported by the FAA and by independent flight trackers (FlightAware, Cirium) are the checkable record of that lag.

Modernization proposals have circulated for years. NextGen, the FAA’s multi-year effort to shift from ground-based radar to satellite-based surveillance and digital communications, has been implemented in stages. Critics of both parties have noted cost overruns and incomplete deployment. A $50 billion emergency request is a new political ask tied to this specific outage; whether Congress appropriates it, and on what conditions, is a future legislative fact, not a present engineering fact.

What this story is not

It is not a finding that any particular administration “caused” the router failure. It is not evidence that the flying public was ever placed at risk of mid-air collision; the FAA and the airlines both state that airborne aircraft landed safely. It is not a completed root-cause analysis. The photograph is a type image of one airport’s control tower taken in 2025; it is not a picture of the El Dorado or Amarillo sites, nor of any passenger queue from the weekend.
