Florida · courts · 1 November 2026

A federal judge dismissed with prejudice President Trump’s $10 billion defamation suit against the Wall Street Journal over its reporting on a 2003 birthday letter.

ObjectiveNews. Target T 100 · N 0 · C 100.

What is agreed across desks

On Saturday, 31 October 2026, U.S. District Judge Darrin P. Gayles of the Southern District of Florida dismissed with prejudice the amended defamation complaint filed by President Donald Trump against Dow Jones, the Wall Street Journal, Rupert Murdoch and related defendants. Reuters, BBC, PBS, and Politico match: the suit sought at least $10 billion; it was the second attempt after an earlier dismissal without prejudice in April; the dismissal with prejudice means the same claim cannot be refiled in that court. The judge found that the complaint failed to plausibly allege “actual malice”—that the Journal knew the statements were false or acted with reckless disregard for their truth—the standard required for a public-figure plaintiff.

The underlying article, published in July 2025, reported on a letter bearing Trump’s name that appeared in a 2003 birthday album compiled for Jeffrey Epstein’s 50th birthday. The letter was described as containing suggestive language and a drawing. Trump has denied writing or signing it. Gayles wrote that the Journal’s investigation was “thoughtful and detailed,” that the complaint was “grasping at straws” to manufacture flaws, and that ignoring a denial does not by itself establish actual malice. The ruling did not decide whether Trump authored the letter or whether the article’s statements were true or defamatory.

What is only a quote

Trump’s prior statements that the story was “false, malicious, and defamatory” are the plaintiff’s characterization. A spokesperson’s comment that the president would continue to press the lawsuit or appeal is a party statement, not a court outcome. The Journal’s assertion that it stands behind its reporting is the defendant’s position. None of these are findings of the court.

Mechanism a reader needs

Under New York Times v. Sullivan and its progeny, a public official or public figure must prove actual malice to recover for defamation. That is a high bar: the plaintiff must show the publisher knew the statement was false or entertained serious doubts about its truth. Mere failure to accept a denial, or ordinary investigative disagreement, is ordinarily insufficient. Gayles had already dismissed the first complaint for failing that standard and allowed amendment; the amended complaint was found to contain the same core defects—conclusory, vague, or inconsistent allegations. Dismissal with prejudice ends the case in that forum on the current theory.

The letter itself has been the subject of separate document releases from Epstein’s estate to congressional investigators. Those releases are independent of the defamation action. The court’s ruling addresses only the legal sufficiency of the complaint; it does not authenticate or refute the letter. Appeals, if any, would go to the Eleventh Circuit. Attorney-fee requests by the defendants were left open.

What this story is not

It is not a determination that the letter is authentic or forged. It is not a finding that the Journal’s reporting was accurate or inaccurate. It is not a criminal matter involving Epstein. The image is a neutral location still unrelated to the parties.
